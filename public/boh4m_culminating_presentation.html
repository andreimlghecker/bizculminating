Old Inefficiencies (Grade 9)

Back in Grade 9 Computer Studies, I had zero systems. Everything was reactive and it showed in my marks.

Habit 1: No Assignment Tracking

I did not track assignments anywhere. I relied entirely on memory to remember due dates, lab submissions, and quiz prep. Relying on your head to remember everything does not work for project management.

Habit 2: Code First, Plan Never

I would jump straight into writing code without drawing logic wireframes or database schemas first. That led to massive late night debugging marathons and dropped efficiency across every unit.

New Efficiencies (Grade 12)

By Grade 12 BOH4M and Computer Science, I rebuilt how I work from the ground up.

Habit 1: Async Project Board

I use an asynchronous project board, basically a digital kanban system, to split school tasks from dev work. Nothing slips through because everything has a column and a deadline.

Habit 2: Architecture Before Code

I write down absolute architecture mockups and database schema plans before writing a single line of code or text. This cut out all the stress and keeps my performance solid.

Performance Tracking & Monitoring

Building my senior tech capstone project, my tech teacher did not just grade the final product. They monitored progress with two strict methods:

First, they ran bi weekly architecture reviews every second Tuesday where my code blueprints were checked line by line.

Second, they set hard checkpoint milestones where specific code modules had to be completely functional before I could move to the next layer.

That oversight caught data logic bugs early, before they broke the whole system.

The Evolution of Work Styles

The Old Way

Handwritten paper worksheets passed around class, massive physical binders for every subject, and strict rows of desks where you sit, listen, and copy notes.

The New Way

Google Classroom for assignments and feedback, online code submissions with version history, and async team docs for group projects, which matches how remote tech teams work.

Two reasons the new way is better: first, it means zero administrative paper clutter, and second, it builds the exact self management habits you need in professional environments, especially the modern remote tech workforce.

Unit 2: Personal Power

Referent & Personal Power

The Tech Department Head at WTCS holds immense personal power over students, and it is earned, not assigned. Three reasons explain this:

First, they have real, practical industry experience which commands respect because they have actually shipped software.

Second, they talk to students like adult junior developers instead of lecturing down at them.

Third, they give up personal after school hours to help unblock complex project bugs.

Because of this, I found myself rewriting code architectures over weekends to meet their high coding standards, and volunteering to optimize lab tech setups so the whole department runs smoother.

Unit 2: Positional Power and Operant Conditioning

Conditioning: Reinforcing Good Habits

Positive Reinforcement

The tech teacher grants special access to premium server hardware and advanced tech tools when project documentation updates are hit perfectly on time. This encourages spotless documentation habits because you learn fast that good docs unlock better tools.

Negative Reinforcement

The teacher removes the requirement for detailed daily written step by step logs once a student consistently shows they can hit major deadlines cleanly. This rewards autonomy because when you prove you are reliable, the micromanagement disappears.

Unit 2: Operant Conditioning11 / 21
Unit 2: Positional Power and Operant Conditioning

Conditioning: Correcting Bad Habits

Positive Punishment

Assigning a student mandatory lab cleanup and cable organizing duties if they leave their local tech workspace messy or unstructured. This instantly stops sloppy lab habits because you clean once and you never want to do it again.

Negative Punishment

Taking away independent lab server access and making the student use highly restricted standalone desktops if they bypass standard safety or deployment guidelines. This eliminates risky shortcuts because restricted access is miserable when you are used to real servers.

Unit 2: Leadership Styles

Autocratic Leadership

The best example at WTCS is the Senior Varsity Sports Coach. There are zero committee meetings and zero debate. One voice calls the shots.

They decide all plays and fitness routines with zero player input because the playbook is the playbook.

They enforce an instant benching rule if someone arrives late, no matter who they are.

Assessment

This is highly effective here because in fast, competitive environments, a single command system cuts out hesitation and keeps discipline tight.

Laissez Faire Leadership

The best example is an Advanced Tech Lab Mentor. They trust you to drive, and only step in when you actually need it.

They completely step back from daily task assignment to let students choose their own software stacks and goals.

They only step in when a student explicitly asks for help with a critical system bug.

Assessment

This is extremely effective for highly driven, tech focused individuals because it forces you to become an independent problem solver.

Team Cohesiveness Case Study

Our tight 4 person software team built an asset tracking app consisting of Andrei, Callum, and two senior peers. Cohesion was very high due to four clear causes:

  • First, the small group size of 4 people cut out any internal communication drag.
  • Second, everyone shared a massive interest in full stack engineering.

Unit 3: Communication Process

Reading Non-Verbal Communication

Signals for Disengagement

Slouched posture facing away from the board shows physical withdrawal from the lesson. Eyes locked downward onto a phone under the desk indicates classic split attention. Restless pen tapping usually means impatience or that they have mentally checked out.

Signals for Active Engagement

Sitting up straight and leaning slightly forward shows presence and readiness. Steady eye contact with the speaker means actively tracking thoughts instead of zoning out. Writing down quick notes in real time means they are processing information, not just hearing it.

Unit 3: Perception and Conflict Resolution

Perception & Solving Conflicts

Self Fulfilling Prophecy

I originally thought business presentations would be dry and academic, which made me expect a boring project. To fight that, I chose to base this strictly on actual software agency workflows and dev metrics. The result was highly engaging and got excellent feedback, which is proof that connecting theories to real tech skills works.

Conflict Resolution using Arbitration

A major argument broke out in a presentation group over scope creep. One side wanted to add messaging features, while the other wanted to lock the code to keep it stable. Because it stalled work, we took it to the Tech Teacher. The teacher acted as an Arbitrator, reviewed the timeline, and made a binding decision to freeze the core code for evaluation and list messages as a future update. That let us immediately get back to work.
