When I got invited to give a guest talk about AI software workflows at a tech meetup on 800 Bay St, I needed to miss an afternoon of class. WTCS does not just let you walk out. There is a strict step by step process.

Old Inefficiencies (Grade 9)

Back in Grade 9 Computer Studies, I had zero systems. Everything was reactive and it showed in my marks.

Habit 1: No Assignment Tracking

I did not track assignments anywhere. I relied entirely on memory to remember due dates, lab submissions, and quiz prep. Relying on your head to remember everything does not work for project management.

Habit 2: Code First, Plan Never

I would jump straight into writing code without drawing logic wireframes or database schemas first. That led to massive late night debugging marathons and dropped efficiency across every unit.

New Efficiencies (Grade 12)

By Grade 12 BOH4M and Computer Science, I rebuilt how I work from the ground up.

Habit 1: Async Project Board

I use an asynchronous project board, basically a digital kanban system, to split school tasks from dev work. Nothing slips through because everything has a column and a deadline.

Habit 2: Architecture Before Code

I write down absolute architecture mockups and database schema plans before writing a single line of code or text. This cut out all the stress and keeps my performance solid.

94% average

Performance Tracking & Monitoring

Building my senior tech culminating project, my tech teacher did not just grade the final product. She monitored progress with two strict methods:

First, she ran bi weekly architecture reviews every second Tuesday where my code blueprints were checked line by line.

Second, she set hard checkpoint milestones where specific code modules had to be completely functional before I could move to the next layer.

That oversight caught data logic bugs early, before they broke the whole system.

Handling Stress & Staying Balanced

The biggest stressor for me is managing a heavy senior high school course load while running a small automation agency and hitting software client deadlines on the side. That is a lot of moving parts.

My fix is blocking off 30 minutes every night with zero screens. I walk, reset my task list for the next day, and shut off client notifications until morning.

It is a simple break, but it stops me from carrying school stress straight into dev work and back again all night.

The Evolution of Work Styles

The Old Way

Handwritten paper worksheets passed around class, massive physical binders for every subject, and strict rows of desks where you sit, listen, and copy notes.

The New Way

Google Classroom for assignments and feedback, online code submissions with version history, and async team docs for group projects, which matches how remote tech teams work.

Two reasons the new way is better: first, it means zero administrative paper clutter, and second, it builds the exact self management habits you need in professional environments, especially the modern remote tech workforce.

Unit 2: Leadership Styles

Autocratic Leadership

The clearest example at WTCS is our Principal, Mr. Koczij. When a school wide decision needs to happen fast, he does not run it like a group vote. He sets the rule and everyone follows it.

He sets non negotiable attendance and lateness policies at the start of the year so there is no confusion about expectations.

During lockdown drills or urgent safety situations, he gives direct instructions over the PA and expects immediate compliance from staff and students.

Assessment

This works because in a whole school environment, one clear authority prevents chaos and keeps 3000 plus students moving safely in the same direction.

Democratic Leadership

My careers teacher, Mr. Jahangirvand, runs his class like a team project. He actually wants input before anything gets finalized.

He runs open class discussions on co op paths, apprenticeships, and university options before students commit to a plan.

He lets students vote on presentation formats and gives each person ownership over their own career portfolio section based on their strengths.

Assessment

This works because students feel heard, which builds trust and makes them put more effort into the final career plan.

Laissez Faire Leadership

My business teacher, Mr. Kwa, takes a hands off approach once he knows you can handle the work. He sets the outcome, then steps back.

He lets students choose their own presentation style, slide layout, and project angle for culminating assignments.

He only steps in when you ask for help or when a group is clearly off track from the rubric.

Assessment

This works for senior students who already know how to manage deadlines because it forces you to become an independent problem solver.

Unit 2: Cohesion and Diversity

Team Cohesiveness Case Study

In gym class, me and my peers got assigned to plan and run a full 30 minute workout for the class. Cohesion was very high because we were locked into one shared goal before the period ended.

  • First, our small group of 5 made it easy to talk through ideas without anything getting lost.
  • Second, everyone wanted the session to feel legit, not like a lazy stretch block.
  • Third, we agreed upfront on 3 stations, a warmup, and a cooldown so the full 30 minutes was covered.
  • Fourth, the class bell gave us a hard deadline that forced us to stop debating and start executing.

Unit 2: Group Development

The 5 Stages of Our Gym Team

1

Stage 1: Forming

We got put in a group, introduced our fitness levels, and sketched a rough 30 minute plan on paper.

2

Stage 2: Storming

We argued over cardio vs strength and whether the warmup should be 5 or 10 minutes.

3

Stage 3: Norming

We split into station leaders, set a rotation timer, and agreed everyone demos their section once.

4

Stage 4: Performing

We ran the full 30 minute workout smoothly with transitions, timing, and clear instructions for the class.

5

Stage 5: Adjourning

We did a quick debrief with our gym teacher, packed up equipment, and moved on to the next unit.

Unit 3: Communication Process

Reading Non Verbal Communication

Signals for Disengagement

During our gym planning huddle, slouched posture on the bleachers showed someone had checked out. Looking at the floor while we debated warmup time meant they were not buying in. Crossed arms usually meant they disagreed but were not going to say it yet.

Signals for Active Engagement

Leaning in while someone explained their station idea showed they were actually listening. Nodding along during the timer breakdown meant they were tracking the plan. Writing notes on the workout sheet meant they were ready to run their part.

Unit 3: Perception and Conflict Resolution

Perception & Solving Conflicts

Self Fulfilling Prophecy

I assumed a student led gym workout would be chaotic, so I went in expecting disorganization. Because of that, I overplanned every detail early. The session actually ran clean, which proved my first assumption wrong once we trusted the process.

Conflict Resolution using Arbitration

Two teammates could not agree on warmup length. One wanted 10 minutes, the other wanted to jump straight into stations. It was eating into prep time, so we asked our gym teacher to step in. She acted as an Arbitrator, set a 7 minute warmup, and told us to move on. We got back to work immediately.
